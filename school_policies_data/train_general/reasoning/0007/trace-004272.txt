To solve this problem, we need to determine the number of trailing zeros in the factorial of a given integer \( N \). Trailing zeros are produced by factors of 10 in the number, which are the product of 2 and 5. Since factorials have more factors of 2 than 5, the number of trailing zeros is determined by the number of times 5 is a factor in the numbers from 1 to \( N \).

### Approach
1. **Understanding Trailing Zeros**: The number of trailing zeros in \( N! \) (N factorial) is determined by the number of times 10 is a factor in \( N! \). Since 10 is the product of 2 and 5, and there are always more factors of 2 than 5 in a factorial, the count of trailing zeros is determined by the count of factors of 5.
2. **Counting Factors of 5**: For each number \( N \), we count the multiples of 5, 25, 125, etc., because each contributes an additional factor of 5. For example, 25 is \( 5^2 \) and contributes two factors of 5.
3. **Summing Contributions**: Sum the contributions from each power of 5 (5, 25, 125, etc.) until the power exceeds \( N \).

### Solution Code
```python
def count_trailing_zeros(n):
    count = 0
    divisor = 5
    while divisor <= n:
        count += n // divisor
        divisor *= 5
    return count

t = int(input())
for _ in range(t):
    n = int(input())
    print(count_trailing_zeros(n))
```

### Explanation
1. **Reading Input**: The code starts by reading the number of test cases \( T \).
2. **Processing Each Test Case**: For each test case, it reads the integer \( N \) and calculates the number of trailing zeros in \( N! \) using the `count_trailing_zeros` function.
3. **Counting Factors of 5**: The function `count_trailing_zeros` initializes a count and a divisor starting at 5. It repeatedly divides \( N \) by the divisor (and subsequent powers of 5) to count how many times 5 is a factor in the numbers from 1 to \( N \). The divisor is multiplied by 5 in each iteration until it exceeds \( N \).

This approach efficiently counts the factors of 5 in \( N! \) and ensures the solution is computed in logarithmic time relative to \( N \), making it efficient even for the upper constraint of \( N = 1000 \).
